## 1. Fraction, decimal and percentage

**Rule:** decimal = numerator ÷ denominator

**Symbols:** The numerator is the selected part. The denominator is the total number of equal parts.

**Use when:** Convert between a fraction, decimal and percentage.

**Conditions:** The denominator is non-zero. A part-of-whole interpretation uses equal parts of the same whole.

**Method:** Divide the numerator by the denominator to obtain a decimal; multiply that decimal by 100 to express a percentage.

**Worked example:** 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375 = 37.5%

**Common mistake:** Do not multiply the fraction by 100 and then label the result as a decimal.

## 2. Percentage of an amount

**Rule:** part = percentage ÷ 100 × whole

**Symbols:** The whole is the original total. The part is the percentage amount.

**Use when:** Find a discount, share or percentage of a quantity.

**Conditions:** Identify the whole used as the percentage base. Convert the percentage to a decimal before multiplying.

**Method:** Divide the percentage by 100; multiply by the whole; state the amount and its units.

**Worked example:** 25% of 80 = 25 ÷ 100 × 80 = 20

**Common mistake:** Do not divide the whole by the percentage.

## 3. Sharing in a ratio

**Rule:** one share = total ÷ total ratio parts

**Symbols:** Total ratio parts is the sum of every number in the ratio.

**Use when:** Split a total into a given ratio.

**Conditions:** For a positive total, ratio parts are non-negative and their sum is positive.

**Method:** Add the ratio parts; divide the total by their sum; multiply this unit share by each ratio part; check that the shares add to the total.

**Worked example:** $60 in the ratio 2:3 gives $24 and $36

**Common mistake:** A 2:3 ratio has five parts, not three.

## 4. Order of operations

**Rule:** brackets → powers → × and ÷ → + and −

**Symbols:** Operations at the same level are completed from left to right.

**Use when:** Evaluate an expression with more than one operation.

**Conditions:** Multiplication and division have equal priority; addition and subtraction have equal priority. Work left to right within each pair.

**Method:** Evaluate brackets; evaluate powers; perform multiplication and division from left to right; perform addition and subtraction from left to right.

**Worked example:** 3 + 4 × 2 = 3 + 8 = 11

**Common mistake:** Do not always multiply before dividing or add before subtracting.

## 5. Unit rate

**Rule:** unit rate = total amount ÷ number of units

**Symbols:** A unit rate compares a quantity with one unit.

**Use when:** Compare prices, speeds or other rates fairly.

**Conditions:** The number of units is positive. Compare rates that describe the same quantities and compatible units.

**Method:** Divide the total quantity by its number of units; label the result per one unit; convert units before comparing rates.

**Worked example:** $18 ÷ 6 items = $3 per item

**Common mistake:** Do not invert dollars per item into items per dollar without changing the label.

## 6. Percentage change

**Rule:** change % = (new − original) ÷ original × 100%

**Symbols:** The original value is the comparison base.

**Use when:** Measure an increase or decrease relative to the starting value.

**Conditions:** The original comparison value is positive in the usual percentage-change model.

**Method:** Subtract original from new; divide the change by original; multiply by 100; interpret a positive result as an increase and a negative result as a decrease.

**Worked example:** 50 to 60: 10 ÷ 50 × 100% = 20%

**Common mistake:** Divide by the original value, not the new value.

## 7. Product of powers

**Rule:** aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ

**Symbols:** a is the common base. m and n are indices.

**Use when:** Multiply powers that have the same base.

**Conditions:** The bases match. For the elementary integer-index rule, avoid undefined powers such as 0⁰ or a negative power of zero.

**Method:** Check that the bases match; add the exponents; keep the common base.

**Worked example:** x³ × x⁴ = x⁷

**Common mistake:** Do not multiply the exponents in a product of powers.

## 8. Quotient of powers

**Rule:** aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ

**Symbols:** a is non-zero and is the common base.

**Use when:** Divide powers that have the same base.

**Conditions:** The bases match and the common base is non-zero.

**Method:** Check the bases; subtract the denominator exponent from the numerator exponent; keep the common base.

**Worked example:** x⁷ ÷ x³ = x⁴

**Common mistake:** Do not subtract the bases or reverse the exponent subtraction.

## 9. Power of a power

**Rule:** (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ

**Symbols:** Multiply the two indices.

**Use when:** Raise an existing power to another power.

**Conditions:** Use the integer-index rule, or a real domain where the powers are defined. A zero base cannot have a negative exponent.

**Method:** Keep the base; multiply the inner and outer exponents; evaluate only if a numerical answer is required.

**Worked example:** (x³)⁴ = x¹²

**Common mistake:** Do not add the exponents for a power of a power.

## 10. Scientific notation

**Rule:** N = a × 10ⁿ, where 1 ≤ |a| < 10

**Symbols:** a is the leading number and n moves the decimal point.

**Use when:** Write very large or very small numbers compactly.

**Conditions:** For a non-zero number, 1 ≤ |a| < 10 and n is an integer.

**Method:** Move the decimal until the coefficient has one non-zero digit before it; count the places; use a positive exponent for large magnitudes and a negative exponent for small ones.

**Worked example:** 4,500,000 = 4.5 × 10⁶

**Common mistake:** Do not leave a coefficient of 45 in standard scientific notation.
