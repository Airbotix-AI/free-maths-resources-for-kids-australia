# Four complete worked examples

These are original editorial examples, not real student work or copied provider questions. Year suggestions are starting points; the child’s actual class may introduce a topic at a different time.

## Foundation–Year 2: make ten

8 + 7 = 8 + 2 + 5 = 10 + 5 = **15**. Split 7 into 2 and 5 because 8 needs 2 to make 10. Check: 15 − 8 = 7. A useful explanation names the split, not just the answer.

## Years 5–6: a fraction of a collection

Find 3/4 of 24 counters. One quarter is 24 ÷ 4 = 6. Three quarters are 3 × 6 = **18**. The unselected quarter has 6 counters; 18 + 6 = 24 checks the whole. An equal-group sketch should show four groups of six, with three groups selected.

## Years 8–9: check a linear rule

For y = 2x + 1:

| x | −1 | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| y | −1 | 1 | 3 | 5 |

Each increase of 1 in x increases y by 2. At x = 3, y = 7, so (3,7) fits and (3,8) does not. A graphing tool can help check the line; the substitution explains why.

## Years 11–12: connect a quadratic’s forms

x² − 5x + 6 = (x − 2)(x − 3). The zeros are **2 and 3**. Completing the square gives (x − 2.5)² − 0.25, so the vertex is **(2.5, −0.25)**. Substituting x = 2.5 gives 6.25 − 12.5 + 6 = −0.25. This is a function example, not a promise that every senior course includes the same content.

Ask the learner to draw or explain, check with a second method, then change one number and predict what changes.
